Why more than one network?

A stream needs a steady upload, every second. One connection, even a fast one, has short moments when it can't give that. A second network covers those moments.

Why one modem isn't enough, even a fast one

Say your modem can upload 50 Mbps, and your stream needs 6 Mbps. That sounds like plenty. But 50 is the best case. Your stream needs its 6 Mbps every second, also in the worst second. Here is what gets in the way.

The speed on the box is the best case

A modem's top speed is what it can do with a perfect signal and a cell tower to itself. A plan's top speed is a best case too. In the US, the official label on a plan also shows a typical upload speed. In Germany, the plan's fact sheet shows an estimated maximum. What you really get depends on the cell tower near you, at that moment.

A cell tower is shared by everyone near it

Each cell tower has a set amount of capacity. Everyone connected to it shares it. On a quiet street, you get a big share. At a concert, a match or a busy station, thousands of phones use the same tower. Then your upload can drop a lot, even with full signal bars.

The signal changes as you move

The farther you are from the tower, the weaker your signal. Walls, buildings and coated glass block a lot of it. Your own body blocks some too, more on the higher 5G bands. So the signal changes with every few steps, and even with how you hold the phone or wear the backpack.

Switching towers pauses the link

As you move, your phone or modem switches from one tower to the next. This is called a handover. During each switch, data stops for a moment. In one study, the pause was about 25 ms (thousandths of a second) on average, and sometimes over 100 ms. In another study, a full switch on 5G took about 170 ms on average. On a motorway, 5G switched towers about every 400 metres.

One pause is short, and a streaming app can wait it out. But on the move it happens again and again, often where the signal is weak anyway.

Some plans wait in line

When a tower is busy, a carrier may serve some plans before others. At a quiet tower, all of them get through. One US carrier's terms say that on certain plans, it "may prioritize your 5G and 4G LTE data behind other traffic" when the cell site is busy. So at a busy place, two people at the same tower can get very different upload. Each plan's terms say whether it has this. More on the limits behind "unlimited".

Upload is the weak direction

Your phone sends with much less power than the tower. So upload fades first: at the edge of coverage, download can still be fast while upload is already slow. A stream is almost all upload. More in Upload first.

Put together: one connection is fast most of the time, but it has short bad moments. With only one connection, your viewers see each of those moments as a freeze or a drop.

How a second network helps

Bonding sends your stream over two or more connections at the same time. It works best when the connections are as different as possible.

Different networks rarely fail at the same moment

Two carriers have their own towers, in different places, on different radio bands, with different crowds. When one is weak, the other is often fine. In a drive test in Denmark, phones on three networks switched towers many times along 130 km. The three networks never switched at the same moment. With two different networks together, the time lost to switching was about 60 times shorter than with one.

Bonding splits the stream into small pieces

Your app cuts the stream into small packets, about 1,300 bytes each. With SRTLA, the open bonding protocol many IRL apps use, the sender sends each packet over the link that has the most room at that moment. A receiver on the internet collects the packets from every link. It puts them back in order and passes one stream on to OBS or the platform.

A faster link gets more packets. A link that loses packets gets fewer. If a packet goes missing, the receiver asks for it again, and the resend can take another link. This is why bonding needs a delay buffer: the SRT latency setting is the time the receiver waits for late and resent packets.

How SRTLA picks a link
  • Room on each link: for every link, the sender counts the packets that are on their way and not yet confirmed. It also keeps a window, a guess of how many packets that link can carry. Each new packet goes to the link with the most free room in its window.
  • Confirmations: the receiver confirms packets on the link they came in on. A link whose packets keep getting confirmed gets a bigger window.
  • Losses: when the receiver reports a lost packet, the window of the link that carried it shrinks. So a link that gets worse quickly gets fewer packets.
  • A silent link: after about 4 to 5 seconds without a reply, the BELABOX sender leaves the link out until it reconnects. An idle link can still answer a small test packet (keepalive).
  • Order and resends are SRT's job. Every packet has a number, so the SRT receiver can put them in order and ask for the missing ones. SRTLA itself doesn't control the bitrate. Your app has to lower it when all links get weak (adaptive bitrate).

This is how the BELABOX SRTLA sender works. Other apps that speak SRTLA may balance their links a little differently.

Links add up

Say each of two links gives 4 Mbps at a busy place. Together they give almost 8. So you can keep a higher bitrate than either link could carry alone. The total is a little less than the sum, because bonding sends some extra data and the links have different delays.

The stream stops only when every link is bad

With one connection, every bad moment reaches your viewers. With bonding, a bad moment on one link only matters when the other links are bad at the same moment. The sender moves the packets to the links that still work.

A sudden drop can still cause a short glitch. That happens when packets that were on the failing link can't be sent again before the latency runs out. A longer SRT latency gives more time for that.

The limits

Two SIMs on the same network help less

Two SIMs from the same carrier use the same towers and share the same crowd. In a full cell or a dead spot, both get weak together. They still help a little with handovers: in the Danish drive test, two phones on the same network rarely switched at the same moment. But two different networks did about twice as well. Many cheaper brands use one of the big networks, so check which one before you buy a second SIM.

It uses more data

Bonding sends a little extra: packet headers, confirmations on every link, and resent packets. In total that's about 10 % on top of your bitrate, a little more with bonding and loss. If your app sends a full copy over each link (redundant mode), the data use grows with every link. And one link may end up carrying most of the stream, so choose each plan as if it carries all of it. Work out your own in the data calculator.

It doesn't fix the camera or the encoder

Bonding only carries the stream. A dark, shaky or blurry picture stays the same. So does a phone or encoder that is too hot or too slow, and a wrong setting in OBS or on the platform.

More modems need more power and cooling

Every phone or modem uses power and gets warm while it sends, more so with a weak signal. Two phones or four modems drain a battery faster, and in a backpack the heat has to get out. Tips to keep a phone cool.

Keep antennas away from USB 3 ports and cables. A USB 3 connection can give off radio noise around 2.4 GHz, which can weaken nearby Wi-Fi and Bluetooth reception.
