Upload first

A stream sends data all the time, so upload is the number that matters most. Plan pages, speed tests and coverage maps mostly show download first.

Your phone talks to a cell tower by radio, in both directions. The tower sends with a lot of power: 80 W or more. Your phone sends with very little: about 0.2 W. Near the tower, both directions work well. Far from the tower, the phone's weak signal fades first. So at the edge of coverage, download can still be fast while upload is already slow.

The newer, higher 5G bands reach less far, so upload gets weak even closer to the tower. In a crowd, many phones share one tower. Then the tower can be full even where the signal is good.

  • Look for a top upload speed. Some plans state one. It is the most you can get. The cell tower near you decides what you really get.
  • Find the upload speed on the plan's official fact sheet, if there is one: where to find it.
  • Pick different networks. If you bond, two SIMs on the same network use the same cell towers and share the same crowd. Many cheaper brands use one of the big networks, so check which.

How much data

Data for one hour, in GB, is your bitrate in Mbps times 0.45. At 6 Mbps that's 2.7 GB an hour.

Sending a stream adds about 10 % on top: packing, packet headers, resent packets and the receiver's replies. Bonding, loss and low bitrates add a little more. The table counts all of it, using measured and worked-out figures.

If you bond, one connection may carry most of the stream. So choose every plan as if it carries the whole stream.

Plans and speed tests count in megabits (Mbps). Data limits count in bytes (MB and GB). One byte is eight bits. More on GB, GiB and Gb.

Work out your own

Enter your stream length, bitrates and how you send it. Your browser does the maths. Nothing is sent or saved.

What the extra is
  • Packing: the app packs video and audio into an MPEG-TS stream of 188-byte packets, each with a 4-byte header (2.2 %), plus about 64 kbit/s of tables, timing and padding. Worked out, and measured with ffmpeg at 3, 6 and 15 Mbit/s; other apps pack a little differently.
  • Packet headers: SRT sends 1,316 bytes of stream per packet, with 16 bytes of SRT, 8 of UDP and 20 of IP header: 3.3 %. Over IPv6 it's 4.9 %. Apps that send part-filled packets add a little more (3.4 % measured). Worked out.
  • Resent packets: SRT sends a lost packet again while there's still time within its latency setting, and again if the resend is lost too. That's loss ÷ (1 − loss) of the stream, and measured about 5 % above it: 2.1 % at 2 % loss, 5.5 % at 5 %. The calculator counts every packet sent, lost or not. Assumption: whether a carrier counts a packet lost on the way isn't published.
  • Out of order: bonded packets take different paths, arrive out of order, and SRT resends some that weren't lost: 0.9 % measured with two links of equal delay. Real links differ in delay and reorder more, so take it as a floor. Measured.
  • Replies: the receiver acknowledges about 24 times a second and the sender answers each, about 14 kbit/s each way. Loss reports grow with loss. With SRTLA, every link gets every acknowledgement, and the receiver confirms each 10 packets on a link with a 72-byte reply, 0.5 % of what that link sends. Measured, and from the receiver's source code.
  • Keepalives: SRTLA keeps each link alive with a tiny packet once a second, echoed back: about 0.3 kbit/s each way per link, or 0.3 MB an hour. From the sender's source code.
  • Redundant sending: every link carries the whole stream, so the data grows with the number of links. A packet only needs resending when every copy is lost. A VPN-based bonding app adds its own tunnel headers, which aren't published, so they aren't counted. Assumption.
  • Error correction (FEC): not counted. SRT can add correction packets, but it's off by default, and the sender code I read doesn't turn it on.
  • Not counted either: the app's own traffic (chat, stats, remote control), other apps on the phone, and encoders that run below or above the set bitrate. With adaptive bitrate the stream drops lower on weak links, so real use is often less. Assumption: the encoder sends its set bitrate.

Measured on one computer with a simulated network (30 ms each way, random loss), 60 seconds per case, with ffmpeg, srt-live-transmit and the BELABOX SRTLA sender and receiver. The calculator's formula comes within one percentage point of all 11 cases, mostly a little above. That was a lab test. A real mobile network can behave differently.

Recording: an MP4 file is the video and audio plus about 7 kbit/s of index. It uses storage on the phone or box. It doesn't use mobile data. Measured with ffmpeg.

GB, GiB and Gb
  • Bits and bytes: bitrates count bits. 1 Mbit/s is 1,000,000 bits a second, and a byte is 8 bits, so 8 Mbit/s fills 1 MB a second.
  • GB: here, 1 GB is 1,000,000,000 bytes (10⁹) and 1 MB is 1,000,000. Plans say GB too.
  • GiB: 1 GiB is 1,073,741,824 bytes (2³⁰), about 7 % more. Some carriers' terms define their GB that way, as 1,024 MB of 1,024 KB. Then your plan holds a little more than this page counts.
  • Gb with a small b is a gigabit: an eighth of a GB.
  • Why a "128 GB" card shows less: it holds 128,000,000,000 bytes. Some systems count in GiB but still write GB, so it shows as about 119 GB, and the file system takes a little more.

Limits behind "unlimited"

"Unlimited" plans often have limits in their terms. There are three kinds:

  • Slow after a limit. After a set amount of data, the speed drops, often to 1 Mbps or much less, until the period renews. "Unlimited" travel eSIMs often do this every day.
  • Slower when the network is busy. Some plans get lower priority, so other customers go first when the network is busy. At a concert, full signal bars can still mean very little upload.
  • Rules on how you use it. Some plans limit or ban hotspot use, using the SIM in a modem or router, or continuous video, however much data is left. More on these below.

A plan that slows down after its data cap looks like a bad connection in the middle of a stream. If the same connection drops at the same point every time, suspect its plan.

Sources

Operators' and sellers' own terms: a travel eSIM fair-use policy, a service description, a plan page, a network management page and plan terms on slowing down when busy

Hotspot, modem and continuous video

These rules can matter more than the amount of data:

  • Hotspot. A plan may cap, slow or ban sharing the phone's data. Sharing over a USB cable counts as hotspot use too.
  • Modem or router. A SIM moved from a phone into a modem or router is a different case. Some unlimited data is only for use on the phone itself, and breaking that rule can end the line.
  • Continuous video. Some terms ban continuous video or webcam broadcasts, however much data you have.
  • Daily reconnects. Some terms end the data connection on their own every 24 hours. In a stream that long, expect a reconnect.

The video limits many plans list are about watching video. The terms I read don't mention a limit on video you send.

Before a long stream, search the plan's terms for "hotspot", "modem", "router", "device" and "continuous". A data plan sold for routers avoids most of these questions.

Read the carrier's own fact sheet

In some countries the law says every carrier must publish a short, standard sheet for each plan and keep it up to date. It is the official source, so read it there.

In the US: the Broadband Facts label

Where: next to the plan on the carrier's website, or behind a link or icon there, and in stores. For the plan you already have, in your online account.

  • Typical Upload Speed and Typical Latency.
  • Data Included with Monthly Price, and Charges for Additional Data Usage with a link that says what happens after.
  • Network Management Policy: a link to how the carrier manages its network. Look there for slowing down when the network is busy.
  • Link to Terms of Contract: hotspot, modem, continuous video and roaming rules aren't on the label itself, so look there.
In Germany: the Produktinformationsblatt

Where: carriers must make it easy to find from the day a plan goes on sale, and point you to it before you sign. Look for a link named "Produktinformationsblatt" on the plan's page. Sheets for plans no longer sold stay in an archive on the carrier's website.

  • Maximum upload: for mobile plans, the sheet gives the estimated maximum speed for download and upload.
  • The data limit: the amount after which the speed drops or you buy more, the speed you get after it, and which services count towards it.
  • Hotspot, router and roaming rules aren't on the sheet. Look in the plan's terms (Leistungsbeschreibung) and price list (Preisliste).

Before you sign, carriers in the EU must also give you a contract summary in a standard form.

Coverage maps

Coverage maps are models: predictions of signal, mostly outdoors. The operators' own maps call themselves approximate. They don't show your upload speed, crowds or your plan, and few show indoors. Modern buildings with coated glass block a lot more signal than older ones.

Germany and the US have official coverage maps. On them, an area counts as 4G covered if the networks' own models expect a minimum download and upload speed at its edge. This assumes you are outdoors and the network is half busy. For upload that minimum is about 0.5 Mbps in Germany and 1 Mbps in the US. A stream needs several times that.

Maps built from people's speed tests show real results. But they only cover places where people happened to test, on their own phones and plans, and sometimes long ago. Use them to compare networks in one place. They can't promise you a speed.

Maps also show 4G or 5G, but not which radio band. A phone or modem without the band an area uses gets less.

Sources

German regulator's map and its parameters, FCC map data specifications and map help, ITU on building loss, the notes on operators' maps (one, two, three), crowd maps' own limits (Ookla, Opensignal, nPerf, CellMapper)

Test a spot before you stream there

  1. Pick two or three networks from the operators' and regulators' maps, then look at a map built from people's speed tests for the same streets.
  2. Test upload at the spot, at the hour you'll stream, with a prepaid SIM or a friend's phone on each network. Run three tests over five minutes and note the lowest.
  3. Stream for 15 minutes at your bitrate and watch each connection. At 6 Mbps that uses about 0.7 GB.
  4. Expect less indoors and in crowds than you measured outside on a quiet day.

Some regulators run free speed-test apps, and the results go into a public map. Examples are Germany's broadband measurement app and the FCC's speed test app in the US.

Sources

The 0.7 GB uses the formula above. German broadband measurement, FCC map help

Abroad: roaming, travel eSIM or local SIM

When you roam with your own SIM, your data usually goes back to your home network first, and only then to the internet. This is called home routing. In a European study (2018), all 16 operators measured did this. It added about 60 ms or more to servers in the country you're in. Far from home it can be much more: in one study, a travel eSIM took 389 ms where a local SIM took 50 ms.

Travel eSIMs often send your data out through another country, through a hub that can be far from both you and home. Several sellers say so on their own help pages.

For a live stream, every packet takes longer, so SRT has less time to resend lost ones within its latency setting. In a bonded stream, the slowest connection sets the pace.

  • Two travel eSIMs that leave through the same hub take the same detour, even on different networks.
  • Some sites guess where you are from your IP address, for example a platform that picks its nearest server. They see the country your data leaves from, which may not be where you are.

Which to pick

  • Home SIM, roaming: simple for a short trip in the EU, at home prices (EU roaming below). The delay grows with the distance from home. Cheap "unlimited" plans can have a lower limit abroad.
  • Travel eSIM: good to arrive with data, or as a backup. Often slow after a daily limit, and often not local.
  • Local SIM: the surest way to get local data. You may need ID to buy one, so buy it before the day you stream. Your phone or modem also needs the bands the local networks use.

Check it: turn off Wi-Fi and any VPN, then open a "what's my IP" page. If it shows another country, your data most likely goes there first. Some eSIMs let you pick that country, so the result is only a hint.

EU roaming (Roam like at home)

With a SIM from an EU country, you can use your plan across the EU at home prices. This covers the EU and EEA, plus Ukraine and Moldova. Switzerland and the UK aren't covered.

Cost and the fair-use limit

Roaming in the EU costs nothing extra. A plan with a set amount of data can use it all abroad, unless that data is very cheap per GB.

For "unlimited" plans and plans with lots of cheap data, the operator may set a fair-use limit for roaming. The limit is at least twice the monthly price without VAT, divided by the EU's wholesale price for 1 GB. The German unlimited plans checked in November 2026 came to about 60–145 GB a month. At 6 Mbps, with the extra from How much data, that's about 20 to 48 hours of streaming.

  • Find your own limit in the contract, the plan's fair-use page or the carrier's app. By law, the contract must explain how the limit works.
  • A discount can lower it, because the limit follows the price you pay.
  • Past the limit, your data keeps working. The operator may charge extra for each GB, at most the wholesale price plus VAT. Cutting off roaming isn't allowed.
  • The wholesale price drops in January 2027, so the limits grow a little.
The four-month check

This check is about living abroad. Normal trips pass it.

The operator may look at your days and your data use over at least four months. If most of your days or most of your data use were at home, all is fine. Every day your phone logs on to the home network counts as a day at home.

A stream abroad uses a lot of data, so your days at home are what count. If both your days and your data use point abroad, the operator must warn you first. You then get at least two weeks to change. Only after that may it charge extra, at the same capped price.

Where your data goes

Inside the EU too, roaming data usually goes to your home network first, which adds delay (see Abroad). The EU rules set prices and quality. They don't say which way your data goes.

The SRT latency setting should cover several round trips, about four on a good network. So if home routing adds 60 ms to the round trip, add about 240 ms of latency. IRL setups often run well above the 120 ms default, so this extra delay often fits. Jitter and loss on the roaming connection matter more. In a bonded stream, the worst connection decides.

Where your relay is matters too. A relay in your home country adds little, because your data goes there anyway. A relay near you abroad makes every packet travel to your home country and back.

Same quality as at home?

The law says yes. Roaming must not be worse than at home where the visited network has the same generation, such as 4G or 5G. Slowing roamers down for business reasons isn't allowed.

Measurements show a gap. The European Commission ran drive tests in 20 countries in 2025. Roamers' latency was worse than at home in 92 % of cases. Upload was worse in 63 %. In a 2024 study in four countries, visited networks gave roamers lower radio priority than their own customers. Industry roaming rules allow that.

The Commission says the exact reasons aren't known yet. National regulators haven't reported any checks of these quality rules so far.

Before you stream in another EU country

  1. Find your roaming limit before the trip. At 6 Mbps, divide it by 3 to get hours of streaming.
  2. Plan for more delay than at home. Raise the SRT latency setting if the stats show many lost or late packets.
  3. Choose your relay: one in your home country adds the least extra delay.
  4. Look up which networks give roamers 5G. Your carrier must publish this for each country and network. If the default network is weak, choose another one by hand.
  5. Test when you arrive. Do the "what's my IP" check from Abroad and run three upload tests. Then stream for 10 minutes and watch each connection.
  6. For a long stay or a big show, add a local SIM. Its data goes to the internet in the country you're in, and it adds another network.
